Decision-Focused Learning for Multistage Stochastic Programs, with Application to Power Network Repair Scheduling

David Krame Kadurha, Alessandro Zocca, Sandjai Bhulai
arXiv arXiv math.OC2026-11-25 AI for optimisation

Decision-focused learning with Fenchel-Young loss for multistage stochastic programs with discrete decisions, applied to power repair scheduling; a clear learning-to-optimise method of interest.

Abstract

Multistage stochastic programs (MSPs) with discrete decisions are hard to solve due to the curse of dimensionality, non-convex value functions, and scenario trees that grow exponentially with the horizon. We develop a decision-focused learning (DFL) framework for MSPs in which a neural network predicts scores for the part of the decision that drives the state transition. Under structural conditions that make this sub-decision enter the stage objective linearly, the network is trained on labels from offline single-scenario problems, using the Fenchel-Young loss with perturbed optimizers, which need only an optimization oracle. We apply the framework to repair scheduling in power networks under stochastic, time-varying component failures, with and without a per-stage repair budget. On a synthetic 5-bus network and on the IEEE 14-bus network at two repair-cost scales, the learned and baseline policies are compared on common evaluation paths. Without the budget, DFL is the cheapest policy on the 5-bus network; on the 14-bus network it does not outperform a near-sighted model-predictive control (MPC) baseline, but it beats repairing every failed component at the higher scale. Under a binding budget, DFL is the cheapest policy evaluated on both networks and at both scales, 14% to 23% below the next-best policy. On the 5-bus network, the mean cost of DFL is 1.7% (without the budget) and 1.8% (with it) above the exact optimum computed by dynamic programming. Within days of computation, sample average approximation yields costlier policies than DFL and the repair-everything heuristic, and stochastic dual dynamic integer programming remains far from convergence. DFL computes its decisions about as fast as the trivial heuristics and orders of magnitude faster than MPC. Since these conditions are not specific to power networks, the framework extends to other MSPs that share this structure.

MeterRouter: Budget-Aware Dynamic Routing for Multi-Agent Systems

Yue Xu, Leikun Yin, Ruiming Min, Yufeng Lai et al.
arXiv arXiv cs.AI2026-11-25 LLM & agentic AI

Budget-aware dynamic routing of multi-agent LLM systems trained with GRPO; directly on agent orchestration and cost optimisation the researcher would want to read.

Abstract

Multi-agent systems (MAS) built on large language models (LLMs) outperform single-agent systems, but inter-agent communication incurs substantial token overhead. While assigning a large LLM to every agent is common, it results in inefficient resource utilization, leaving the system far from the Pareto frontier. The cost of answering a query is set jointly by how many agents are recruited, the role and LLM assigned to each, and communication topology among them. Most existing routers treat cost only implicitly rather than as an explicit optimization constraint. A fixed accuracy-cost preference also cannot express how much a caller is willing to pay for a particular query. We introduce MeterRouter, a budget-aware dynamic router that incrementally synthesizes a collaboration graph by selecting each agent's role, LLM, incoming connections, and termination conditioned on the query and a per-query monetary budget. We initialize the router by imitating successful execution graphs, then jointly refine its decisions with a variant of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) whose reward favors correct answers and penalizes spending and budget overruns. Across five benchmarks and a three-tier LLM pool, MeterRouter is (1) accurate, achieving performance comparable to the strongest MAS; (2) economical, reaching that accuracy at 82% lower cost than the strongest MAS router and extending the accuracy-cost frontier beyond the ceiling of single-agent systems; and (3) controllable, adapting its spending to the specified budget while rarely exceeding it.

AutoRSP: An AI Agent for Radar Sensing Pipeline Design

Zhi Zheng, Xincheng Xie, Avery Gump, Sacha Jungerman et al.
arXiv arXiv cs.NI2026-11-25 LLM & agentic AI

An LLM agent that automatically designs radar sensing pipelines using simulation and hardware feedback in place of an LLM judge; agentic design loops are adjacent to the researcher's interests.

Abstract

Radar sensing demands a pipeline that is well-thought-out across hardware and software. We ask whether an AI agent can automatically design radar sensing pipelines at a fraction of the time, cost, and expertise that current practice needs. We present AutoRSP, an agent that embeds radar-system-level structure through sub-agents mapped to five stack layers, a code-based representation of every layer with explicitly modeled cross-layer dependencies. Feedback for the agentic search process comes from a pipeline of realistic RF simulation, on-sensor emulation, and execution on real edge devices, rather than from an LLM judge. Because such feedback can be slower than an LLM call, we present methods to efficiently optimize agentic loops. We evaluate AutoRSP on robotic imaging, automotive perception, and vital sign sensing. It produces executable pipelines with performance comparable to or better than human-expert pipelines and closed-source agents. AutoRSP's harness, tools, and benchmarks will be released to bridge the rising gap in agentic research abilities outside of frontier labs.

Crucible: Evaluating LLM Agents in Executable Chemistry Research Environments

Yinjun Liu, Xinye Li, Ruisen Peng, Lang Gao et al.
arXiv arXiv cs.AI2026-11-25 LLM & agentic AI

Benchmark of LLM agents using tools in executable chemistry environments; relevant agent evaluation with simulators, though domain is chemistry.

Abstract

Large language models (LLMs) perform well on chemistry knowledge benchmarks, which mainly assess question answering and isolated tasks. Chemical research, however, requires using specialized tools and interpreting their outputs to obtain chemically valid results. To address this gap, we introduce Crucible Bench to evaluate this ability across all four stages of the design-make-test-analyze (DMTA) cycle. The benchmark contains 1,078 tasks in 12 task families curated from 18 published datasets, covering molecular design, synthesis planning, experimental execution, closed-loop optimization, simulation, and analytical characterization. Agents access 75 domain-specific tools through the Model Context Protocol in seven isolated environments. Sandboxed graders verify the resulting synthesis routes, experimental protocols, and other outputs. Laboratory tasks use robotic simulators or previously measured experimental data. We evaluate 14 LLMs as agents and find that the best achieves an overall score of 0.545, with synthesis planning and closed-loop optimization emerging as the hardest domains. Overall performance does not predict optimization performance well: the model ranked twelfth overall achieves the highest score in closed-loop optimization. Error analysis also shows that agents often submit chemically invalid results despite running their tools successfully. Stronger models benefit more from tool access, but reliable use of chemical software remains a challenge that Crucible Bench can track as models improve.

Do We Still Need Reinforcement Learning When We Can Read the World Model?

Mingchao Zhang, Miduo Cui, Kangze He, Yuanjun Li et al.
arXiv arXiv cs.LG2026-11-25 LLM & agentic AI

LLM coding agents synthesize control policies from readable world models instead of RL; agentic decision-making close to the researcher's interests.

Abstract

Reinforcement learning has achieved remarkable success in sequential decision-making, but learning effective policies often requires substantial interaction experience. Model-based reinforcement learning alleviates this dependence by leveraging world models, which are typically used to support planning or policy optimization. In this paper, we investigate an alternative paradigm in which autonomous LLM-based coding agents directly synthesize control policies by reasoning over readable world models. By examining environment source code, these agents translate environmental knowledge into executable decision rules and iteratively refine their policies using online feedback or offline data, without task-specific RL training. Experiments across diverse decision-making benchmarks show that synthesized policies match or outperform conventional RL methods while requiring substantially less experience. Further analyses reveal interpretable decision rules and how they evolve through feedback-driven refinement. Comparisons with policies generated without source code access and experiments under challenging conditions further demonstrate the benefits of readable world models. These findings suggest that direct policy synthesis can reduce reliance on trial and error, challenging the necessity of task-specific RL training.
